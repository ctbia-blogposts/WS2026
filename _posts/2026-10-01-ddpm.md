## The question

A photograph contains decisions at many scales: which objects exist, where their boundaries lie, and what textures fill the regions between them. A generator must make these decisions jointly. A denoiser seems to have an easier job: most of the image is already there.

**DDPM asks whether generation can be built out of denoising problems.** Make those problems progressively harder during training; solve them in the opposite order during sampling. At the hardest noise level, almost no information about the original image survives. The denoiser must rely on what it has learned about the data distribution.

Ho, Jain, and Abbeel's _Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models_ is not the first diffusion model. Its contribution is a particularly effective way to parameterize and train the reverse process <d-cite key="ho2020denoising"></d-cite>. The distinction matters: the forward chain supplies mathematical structure, but the experimental breakthrough depends on the objective used to learn its reversal.

This review follows three questions. What makes the corruption process tractable? Why is predicting noise enough to learn a generative model? And which experiments distinguish a good explanation of DDPM from the slogan “add noise, then remove it”?

## A process we can solve

### Corruption is fixed; generation is learned

Write $x_0$ for a training image, scaled to $[-1,1]$. Choose a sequence of small variances $\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_T$. The forward transition is

$$
q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})=
\mathcal N\!\left(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\,\beta_t I\right).
$$

The square-root factor slightly attenuates the image before adding noise. Without attenuation, the variance would keep growing. With it, the process approaches a standard Gaussian under an appropriate schedule. The paper uses $T=1000$ and a linear schedule from $\beta_1=10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T=0.02$.

### A thousand-step chain has a one-step training formula

Define $\alpha_t=1-\beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^t\alpha_s$, with $\bar\alpha_0=1$. Composing Gaussian transitions gives

$$
\underbrace{x_t}_{\text{noisy observation}}
=
\underbrace{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}_{\text{remaining signal}}
+
\underbrace{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon}_{\text{noise}},
\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).
$$

This is the main computational convenience: **sample any training timestep directly**. Training does not need to unroll a thousand forward transitions. The network sees different noise levels across minibatches, not a long chain attached to each gradient update.

<aside class="seminar-sidenote" aria-label="Terminal prior approximation">
<p><strong>Almost, not exactly, pure noise.</strong> At t=1000, a small signal remains. Starting from a standard Gaussian is an approximation to the terminal corruption distribution.</p>
</aside>

<details class="seminar-derivation" markdown="1" id="ddpm-forward-derivation">
<summary>Derivation: why we can sample any noise level directly</summary>

Write one forward step as

$$
x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,z_t,
\qquad z_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I),
$$

where $z_t$ is independent of the previous forward noise. Suppose the preceding state, conditioned on $x_0$, has mean $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0$ and covariance $(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})I$. Scaling it and adding independent Gaussian noise gives

$$
\mathbb E[x_t\mid x_0]
=\sqrt{\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0
=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(x_t\mid x_0)
&=[\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})+\beta_t]I\\
&=(1-\bar\alpha_t)I.
\end{aligned}
$$

The base case is $t=1$, where the result is exactly the chosen forward kernel. By induction, every marginal is Gaussian with these parameters. We can therefore draw $x_t$ directly using **one new standard Gaussian** $\epsilon$ and the closed-form equation above. This $\epsilon$ represents the accumulated corruption at a chosen timestep; it is not necessarily the single-step noise $z_t$.

</details>

## What the network learns

### The target is known; the answer is ambiguous

For each example, sample $x_0$, a uniform timestep $t$, and independent Gaussian noise $\epsilon$. Construct $x_t$ with the formula above. A timestep-conditioned network $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)$ predicts the noise, using the loss

$$
L_{\mathrm{simple}}
=\mathbb E_{x_0,t,\epsilon}
\left[\left\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right\|_2^2\right].
$$

We know the target because we sampled it. But the network does **not** receive $x_0$. Many clean images and noise realizations can explain the same $x_t$. With unlimited capacity and data, squared-error regression learns the conditional expectation $\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t,t]$, not the exact hidden noise of every example.

<aside class="seminar-sidenote" aria-label="Regression optimum qualification">
<p><strong>A population optimum.</strong> The conditional-mean statement assumes the expected loss is minimized with enough capacity. A finite trained network only approximates that predictor.</p>
</aside>

### Inside the denoiser

The probabilistic model tells us what to predict, but not how to build the predictor. DDPM uses a **timestep-conditioned U-Net**: an encoder reduces spatial resolution, a decoder restores it, and skip connections carry encoder features into the corresponding decoder levels. The input is the noisy image $x_t$; the output is a noise estimate with the same spatial dimensions and channels.

The encoder gives later layers a larger spatial context. The skip connections preserve local information that the decoder would otherwise have to reconstruct from a compressed feature map. These are complementary roles: estimating the noise in one pixel can depend on both nearby edges and the broader object around it.

**The timestep is a conditioning signal, not another image channel.** Sinusoidal features of $t$ pass through a small multilayer perceptron. Each residual block projects this embedding to its channel dimension and broadcasts it across spatial positions before adding it to the image features. The same weights can therefore behave differently at high and low noise.

<p><strong>Time is not a prompt.</strong> This embedding specifies the corruption level. The original unconditional model has no text-conditioning pathway; its attention relates image features.</p>
</aside>

Self-attention provides an additional route for information to move between spatial locations. Unlike a local convolution, an attention operation can directly relate distant positions within a feature map. The released model uses attention at 16×16 and in its bottleneck, rather than at every spatial resolution.

For a closer implementation reading, the encoder has two residual blocks at each resolution; the decoder has an additional block per level to consume its skip tensors. The bottleneck is residual–attention–residual. The 32×32 model has four resolutions; the paper’s 256×256 models have six. Thus the figure describes a specific model scale, while the underlying construction is reusable.

The architecture does not itself implement the reverse diffusion equation. It produces $\epsilon_\theta$; the sampler computes $\mu_\theta$ and adds the prescribed random term outside the U-Net. Keeping this boundary clear explains why the same network can be queried once during a training update and repeatedly during generation.

This averaging is not a flaw in the derivation. It is what squared-error prediction should do. The mistake would be to treat a posterior mean as a full sample from the clean-data distribution. Generation needs a sequence of transitions, with uncertainty handled along the way.

### Why this is also score estimation

Let $q_t(x_t)$ be the distribution of noisy data at timestep $t$. Its _score_ is the gradient $\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t)$: a local direction toward higher density. For the Gaussian corruption above,

$$
\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t)
=-\frac{\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t,t]}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}.
$$

Thus an optimal noise predictor estimates the score after rescaling and changing sign. The relevant object is the score of the **noisy-data marginal**, not the score conditioned on one clean image. This connects DDPM to multi-scale denoising score matching <d-cite key="song2019generative"></d-cite>.

The distinction gives the experiment a second interpretation. When the two clouds overlap, the learned direction reflects their combined probability density; it cannot simply point back to a uniquely determined source.

<details class="seminar-derivation" markdown="1" id="ddpm-score-derivation">
<summary>Derivation: from noise prediction to the noisy-data score</summary>

For a fixed clean source $x_0$, the corruption distribution is Gaussian. Its log-density gradient is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0)
&=-\frac{x_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\\
&=-\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The marginal $q_t(x_t)$ averages these Gaussian densities over clean sources. Differentiating that mixture and dividing by its density gives a posterior-weighted average of the component scores:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t)\\
&\quad=\mathbb E\!\left[
\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0)
\,\middle|\, x_t,t
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the Gaussian score yields

$$
\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t)
=-\frac{\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t,t]}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}.
$$

Squared-error noise regression targets the conditional expectation in the numerator. The identity is exact for that optimal predictor; replacing it with a learned $\epsilon_\theta$ gives an **estimated** score. This is why a training target attached to one clean image can teach a quantity describing the entire noisy-data distribution.

</details>

### Where the loss comes from—and what was changed

The generative model is a reverse Markov chain starting at $p(x_T)=\mathcal N(0,I)$. A variational bound on the negative log-likelihood can be decomposed as

$$
L_{\mathrm{VLB}}
= L_T + \sum_{t=2}^{T}L_{t-1}+L_0,
$$

where $L_T$ compares the terminal corruption with the prior, $L_0$ is a reconstruction/decoder term, and each intermediate $L_{t-1}$ compares a true conditional posterior with the learned reverse transition.

The useful posterior is $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)$. It is Gaussian and available during training because we know $x_0$. This does **not** make the unconditional reverse $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$ known: that distribution still depends on the data.

During training, we also know the clean image $x_0$. Conditioning on it gives $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)$, which is exactly Gaussian for the chosen forward process. Small steps make a Gaussian approximation to the first distribution more accurate in this example; they do not make it exactly Gaussian in general.

With fixed reverse variance $\sigma_t^2$, the intermediate KL term becomes a weighted noise-prediction error:

$$
L_{t-1}
=\mathbb E\!\left[
\underbrace{\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)}}_{w_t}
\left\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right\|^2
\right]+C.
$$

DDPM's simplified objective drops these timestep-dependent weights. **It is not the original likelihood bound with a shorter name.** It changes which denoising problems receive emphasis. The paper's experiments show that this choice matters more for sample quality than merely replacing mean prediction with noise prediction. <d-footnote>The displayed KL expression applies to the intermediate Gaussian transitions. The final decoder handles discrete image intensities separately; treating its term as the same continuous Gaussian KL would obscure a boundary condition in the likelihood calculation.</d-footnote>

## From prediction to generation

### Noise prediction determines the reverse mean

The paper parameterizes a Gaussian reverse transition with a learned mean and fixed variance. The noise predictor sets that mean through

$$
\mu_\theta(x_t,t)
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}
\left[x_t-
\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,
\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right].
$$

It is tempting to read this as “subtract the noise to get the clean image.” That is not what this formula does: it computes a mean for **one step**, from $t$ to $t-1$. Predicting the fully clean image is a different expression,

$$
\hat x_0(x_t,t)
=\frac{x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}.
$$

At very high noise, the denominator is small; errors in noise prediction can become large errors in $\hat x_0$. Sampling avoids relying on a single such prediction as the final answer.

### A sample is a stochastic trajectory

Start with $x_T\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$. For $t=T,\ldots,1$, compute

$$
x_{t-1}=\mu_\theta(x_t,t)+\sigma_t z,
\qquad
z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\ \text{if }t>1;
\quad z=0\ \text{if }t=1.
$$

The added randomness is part of the model, not an implementation accident. Repeated small stochastic steps can represent alternatives that a single denoising mean would average together. The fixed variance choices studied in the paper include $\beta_t$ and the forward posterior variance

$$
\tilde\beta_t
=\beta_t\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}.
$$

Each transition reuses the [same timestep-conditioned U-Net](#inside-the-denoiser). Noise level changes its task; it does not select a different network.

A practical implementation should keep these roles separate. The compact pseudocode below shows the distinction; it omits batching, optimizer details, clipping conventions, and the discrete decoder used for likelihood evaluation.

```python
# Training: one noise level per example.
t = uniform_integer(1, T)
epsilon = randn_like(x0)
xt = sqrt(alpha_bar[t]) * x0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t]) * epsilon
loss = mean_squared_error(epsilon_theta(xt, t), epsilon)

# Sampling: the full reverse chain.
x = randn(image_shape)
for t in range(T, 0, -1):
    predicted_noise = epsilon_theta(x, t)
    mean = (x - beta[t] / sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t]) * predicted_noise) / sqrt(alpha[t])
    z = randn_like(x) if t > 1 else zeros_like(x)
    x = mean + sigma[t] * z
```

The first two FIDs are close. The substantial change appears when the noise-prediction objective is reweighted. The slightly worse likelihood bound under that objective is not a contradiction: likelihood evaluates probability assigned to all data details, while FID compares feature-space statistics of generated and reference images.

The headline FID uses training-set reference statistics; the paper also reports $5.24$ against the test set. These are different evaluation setups, not competing estimates of one identical number. FID depends on the feature extractor, reference population, and sampling protocol. It is evidence of image-distribution quality—not proof of diversity, factual correctness, or clinical validity.

The paper's rate–distortion analysis provides a complementary view: much of the reduction in reconstruction error occurs at low coding rates. It connects the chain to progressive lossy decompression. ## What carries forward

### Credit the idea and the particular recipe separately

Sohl-Dickstein et al. introduced diffusion probabilistic modeling before DDPM <d-cite key="sohl2015deep"></d-cite>. Song and Ermon developed generative modeling with noise-conditioned scores <d-cite key="song2019generative"></d-cite>. DDPM connects these perspectives and demonstrates an effective combination of parameterization, objective, architecture, and schedule.

Later work helps identify which parts were essential and which were choices. DDIM changes sampling trajectories while retaining the DDPM training objective <d-cite key="song2021ddim"></d-cite>. Improved DDPM revisits schedules, objectives, and learned variances <d-cite key="nichol2021improved"></d-cite>. The original paper's unstable learned-variance experiments are therefore a result about its configuration, not a general argument that variances must always be fixed.

### Three limits worth keeping in view

**Compute moves into sampling.** Easy-to-construct training targets do not imply cheap generation. The original sampler's thousand sequential network calls are a central limitation. Later acceleration methods address it; they should not be silently credited to the 2020 model.

**A Gaussian transition is an approximation to the reverse.** The forward kernel is exactly Gaussian, but reversing a data-dependent mixture need not yield a single Gaussian at finite step size. Small corruption steps make the modeling choice more reasonable; model error and accumulated sampling error remain.

**A good natural-image generator is not yet a medical model.** DDPM's experiments do not establish diagnostic fidelity, preservation of rare pathology, or calibrated uncertainty in medical data. A model can generate plausible anatomy while changing a clinically decisive detail. For this seminar, the useful transfer is the modeling framework; a biomedical application needs its own validation against the intended task and population.

{% include seminar/callout.liquid kind="critical" content="The noise used by DDPM is a chosen mathematical corruption process. It should not automatically be interpreted as scanner noise, a physical acquisition model, or a clinically harmless image transformation." %}

A productive follow-up question is therefore specific: when diffusion is used to reconstruct or edit medical images, which measurements constrain the sample, and which structures are supplied by the learned prior? The two-point experiment shows the issue in miniature. Ambiguous observations leave room for multiple explanations; a plausible output alone cannot tell us which explanation is correct.

## Takeaways

1. **The forward process buys tractability.** Its Gaussian marginals let us construct arbitrary noise levels without simulating a training chain.
2. **Noise prediction learns a distribution-dependent quantity.** The optimal predictor is a conditional expectation, equivalent to a rescaled noisy-data score—not perfect recovery of each injected noise realization.
3. **The objective is part of the contribution.** The simplified loss changes timestep weighting; the ablation, not just the best FID, explains why that choice matters.
4. **A sample is more than a denoised estimate.** Stochastic reverse transitions turn a shared network's predictions into a generative trajectory, at substantial sequential cost.

DDPM is compelling because the mathematical and empirical stories meet: an analytically convenient corruption process becomes an effective image generator when paired with a carefully chosen learning problem. Understanding that meeting point is more useful than memorizing the sampling equation—and more transferable than treating every later diffusion capability as if it were already in this paper.
